# Video briefs: your first six videos

The niche is hidden places of the Great Lakes region (see [02-niche-and-channel.md](02-niche-and-channel.md)).

I checked the main facts below by web search on 11 November 2026. Where sources disagree, the brief gives the range. I read search summaries, not every full article, so **confirm each fact in the original source before you record**, and use the wording "about" or "at least" where figures vary.

## How to choose video 1

Start with brief 1. It has a single dramatic event, a clear mystery, a scientific answer, and a major US city in the title. If you prefer another, pick the one you can find the most pictures for.

## 1. The wave that hit Chicago on a calm morning (1954)

**Titles**
- In 1954 a Wave Hit Chicago on a Calm Morning. Nobody Saw It Coming
- The Day Lake Michigan Sent a Tsunami Into Chicago
- Lake Michigan Has Tsunamis. One Killed People in Chicago in 1954

**Thumbnail:** an old black-and-white pier with a wall of water drawn over it, the text "CHICAGO, 1954", and "NO WARNING" in a smaller label.

**Opening lines**
> On the morning of June 26, 1954, the storm had already passed. The sky over Chicago was clearing, the lake looked calm, and fishermen were lined up along the piers. Then, with no warning at all, the water rose. A wave as high as a wall swept along miles of shoreline and pulled people off the rocks. At the time it was given the wrong name. What it really was is something most people think only happens in oceans.

**Outline**
1. The morning: the storm that passed, the people on the piers
2. The wave arrives: where it hit and how far along the shore
3. The dead and the rescues
4. What people called it at the time, and why that was wrong
5. What it really was: a wave made by weather, not an earthquake
6. Ten days later it happened again, and nobody died. Why?
7. How often this happens on the Great Lakes
8. Can it be forecast today? What to do if the water suddenly pulls back

**Checked facts**
- Date: June 26, 1954. A second wave came on July 6, 1954, and caused no deaths because warnings had been given.
- Height: sources give 6 to 10 feet.
- Deaths: most sources say 7 or 8. Say "at least seven".
- It reached from Wilmette Harbor in the north to North Avenue Beach.
- It was first reported as a seiche. Scientists now class it as a meteotsunami, driven by a fast-moving line of storms.

**Still to check:** the exact piers and the names of victims; how many such waves the Great Lakes get each year (one scientist is quoted as saying about 100).

**Pictures:** Chicago newspaper archives, National Weather Service Chicago history page, NOAA diagrams of meteotsunamis, Google Earth fly-over of the shoreline.

## 2. The salt mine under Detroit

**Titles**
- There Is a City-Sized Salt Mine 1,200 Feet Under Detroit
- Detroit Is Sitting on 1,500 Acres of Tunnels. They Are Still Being Dug
- The Hidden City Under Detroit That Most People There Have Never Seen

**Thumbnail:** a cut-away drawing with the Detroit skyline on top and white tunnels far below, the text "1,200 FEET DOWN".

**Opening lines**
> Drive through southwest Detroit and you will pass factories, rail yards and ordinary streets. Nothing tells you what is underneath. More than a thousand feet below the pavement there is a second city: about a hundred miles of roads, cut through solid salt. It is over a century old. And trucks are still driving through it today.

**Outline**
1. What is down there, in numbers
2. Why there is salt under Michigan: an ancient sea
3. The discovery, and the years it took to sink the first shaft
4. Life in the mine: how machines get down a narrow shaft
5. The boom years and the closure in 1983
6. The reopening, and what the salt is used for now
7. Why almost nobody is allowed in
8. What happens to a mine like this in the future

**Checked facts**
- Depth: most sources say about 1,200 feet; some say 1,100 or 1,160.
- Size: about 1,500 acres with roughly 100 miles of roads, mostly under Dearborn and River Rouge.
- Salt was found in 1895. Shaft work began in 1906. One source gives 1910 as the opening year.
- Production stopped in 1983. The Detroit Salt Company bought the mine in 1997 and restarted it (one source says 1998).

**Still to check:** what the salt is sold for today; how equipment is taken down; the age of the salt beds.

**Pictures:** the Detroit Salt Company's own published photos (ask permission), Library of Congress, state geological survey diagrams, a simple drawn cross-section.

## 3. The 9,000-year-old hunting ground at the bottom of Lake Huron

**Titles**
- There Is a 9,000-Year-Old Hunting Ground at the Bottom of Lake Huron
- Scientists Found Stone Walls Under Lake Huron. People Built Them
- Lake Huron Was Once Dry Land. The Proof Is Still Down There

**Thumbnail:** a diver's light on a line of stones on the lake floor, the text "BUILT BY PEOPLE", and "9,000 YEARS AGO" below.

**Opening lines**
> Sixty miles out from the Michigan shore, in the middle of Lake Huron, there is a ridge of rock on the lake floor. Nine thousand years ago it was not under water. It was a strip of dry land running between two lakes, and herds of caribou crossed it every year. People were waiting for them. The stone lanes and blinds those hunters built are still there, in the dark, where they left them.

**Outline**
1. The ridge today: where it is and how deep
2. The Great Lakes after the ice: lower water and a land bridge
3. The idea that someone should look there
4. What the sonar and the divers found
5. How the hunt worked: lanes, blinds and the seasons
6. Why the lake preserved it so well
7. What else may be down there
8. What this changes about the first people of the region

**Checked facts**
- A 2014 study led by John O'Shea of the University of Michigan described a 9,000-year-old caribou hunting structure under Lake Huron, published in the journal PNAS.
- It lies on the Alpena-Amberley Ridge, which runs roughly between Alpena, Michigan, and Point Clark, Ontario.
- The main site is known as Drop 45. V-shaped hunting blinds near it face the direction caribou moved in autumn.
- Over nearly ten years the team identified more than 60 drive lanes and hunting blinds.
- The site is about 60 miles by boat from Alpena.

**Still to check:** the depth of the site (not given in the sources I found); the date of the first discovery (reports go back to 2009).

**Pictures:** University of Michigan press images (ask permission or use with credit as their terms allow), NOAA lake-floor maps, your own animated map of ancient lake levels.

## 4. The year Niagara Falls was turned off (1969)

**Titles**
- In 1969 Engineers Turned Off Niagara Falls. Here Is What Was Underneath
- The 5 Months Niagara Falls Ran Dry
- Why the US Army Shut Off Niagara Falls and Then Changed Its Mind

**Thumbnail:** a real 1969 photograph of the dry rock face, the text "TURNED OFF", and "1969" in a label.

**Opening lines**
> In June 1969, one of the most famous waterfalls on Earth stopped. Where millions of gallons had poured every minute, there was bare, dry rock, and engineers walking where the river used to be. It was not a drought and it was not an accident. The United States Army had built a dam and switched off the American Falls on purpose. What they found changed their plan completely.

**Outline**
1. The problem: the falls were burying themselves in fallen rock
2. The plan to stop the water
3. Building the dam in three days
4. What the dry riverbed revealed
5. What the engineers did while the water was off
6. The choices: remove the rock, or leave it
7. The decision, and the day the water came back
8. What is happening to the falls today

**Checked facts**
- A cofferdam about 600 feet long, made of about 28,000 tons of earth and rock, was started on June 9, 1969, and finished on June 12.
- It sent the water over to the Horseshoe Falls.
- Engineers drilled more than 40 test holes and fixed instruments to the rock.
- Removing the fallen rock was costed at $1 million to $10 million and judged too expensive. The public also favoured leaving the falls alone.
- The dam was removed on November 25, 1969. Sources call the shutdown five or six months.

**Still to check:** what was found on the riverbed (coins and human remains are widely reported; confirm in the Army Corps account).

**Pictures:** US Army Corps of Engineers photographs from 1969, Library of Congress.

## 5. The storm that sank 12 ships in four days (1913)

**Titles**
- The 1913 Storm That Sank 12 Ships on the Great Lakes in 4 Days
- 8 Ships Vanished on Lake Huron in 6 Hours
- The Great Lakes Storm So Violent They Called It a Hurricane

**Thumbnail:** an upturned steel hull in heavy seas (a real 1913 photograph exists), the text "12 SHIPS", and "4 DAYS" below.

**Opening lines**
> In November 1913, two storms met over the Great Lakes and became one. For four days it blew with the force of a hurricane, and it brought blinding snow. Steel freighters longer than a football field went out into it and were never seen again. In one six-hour stretch on Lake Huron, eight ships went down and 187 people died. It is still the deadliest storm in the history of the lakes.

**Outline**
1. The Great Lakes fleet in 1913 and why captains kept sailing in November
2. Two weather systems merge
3. The warnings, and why they were not enough
4. The first losses
5. The night of November 9 on Lake Huron
6. The morning after: the ship floating upside down
7. The count, and the bodies that came ashore
8. What changed afterward in forecasting and ship design

**Checked facts**
- Dates: November 7 to 10, 1913 (one weather service account says to November 11). The worst was November 9 and 10.
- Deaths: more than 250; some sources say up to 300.
- Ships: most sources say 12 sank and at least 19 more were wrecked or stranded.
- On Lake Huron, eight ships and 187 lives were lost in about six hours on the evening of November 9.
- A weather service computer study found frequent waves of about 36 feet on Lake Huron.

**Still to check:** that it remains the deadliest Great Lakes storm on record; which wrecks have been found and when; the name of the ship found floating upside down.

**Pictures:** Library of Congress, National Weather Service 1913 retrospective, historical society collections.

## 6. The one lock the American steel industry depends on

**Titles**
- Most of America's Iron Ore Passes Through One Lock in Michigan
- If This One Lock Fails, US Steel Production Stops Within Weeks
- The Single Weak Point in the American Steel Industry

**Thumbnail:** a satellite view of the locks with one circled in red, the text "ONE LOCK".

**Opening lines**
> Between Lake Superior and the rest of the Great Lakes there is a drop of twenty-one feet. Every ship carrying iron ore from the mines of Minnesota has to get past it. There are locks to lift and lower them. But the largest ships, the ones that carry most of the ore, fit through only one. A government study asked what would happen if that single lock closed for six months. The answer was eleven million jobs.

**Outline**
1. The rapids at Sault Ste. Marie and why locks were built
2. How a lock works, in thirty seconds
3. The giant lake freighters and why they outgrew the other lock
4. What moves through: ore, and where it goes
5. The government study and its numbers
6. Why a second large lock took decades to approve
7. The new lock: where it stands now
8. How one piece of concrete became a national risk

**Checked facts**
- The Poe Lock is the only one of the Soo Locks that can take the 1,000-foot freighters.
- Ships carrying about 88% of the commercial cargo cannot use the smaller MacArthur Lock.
- More than 80 million tons a year pass through the locks.
- A Department of Homeland Security study of a six-month closure estimated about 11 million jobs lost, $1.1 trillion in lost activity, and about 75% of US integrated steel production stopping within two to six weeks.

**Still to check:** the height of the drop (I gave 21 feet from memory); the current state and finish date of the new lock (my sources were from 2015 to 2017, so this part is out of date).

**Pictures:** US Army Corps of Engineers Detroit District, NASA satellite images, Google Earth fly-over.
