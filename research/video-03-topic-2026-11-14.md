## The topic: Superior Shoal

A 20-square-mile underwater mountain of ancient lava in the middle of Lake Superior, 50 miles north of Copper Harbor, Michigan. Its top is only 21 feet below the surface, in an otherwise deep part of the lake. Fishermen knew of it for generations, but it was only officially charted in 1929. It has been suggested as the cause of the loss of the French minesweepers Inkerman and Cerisoles (November 1918), the disappearance of the SS Bannockburn (1902) and even the Edmund Fitzgerald (1975). (Wikipedia, "Superior Shoal", citing the US Lake Survey and shipwreck histories; to be confirmed against primary sources while scripting.)

Why it fits: a hidden place, danger, a mystery with famous lost ships, the "hiding" title pattern (43% breakout rate in our title research), Bannockburn and Fitzgerald are both in the news cycle this month, and only one big video covers it.

3. **There is a named, published theory linking the two.** Paul Hainault, a retired mechanical engineering professor at Michigan Technological University, argues that the Edmund Fitzgerald grounded on Superior Shoal at about 9:30 a.m. on 10 November 1975. In his account, a seiche raised and lowered the lake that day and helped the shoal tear open the hull. (Wikipedia, "SS Edmund Fitzgerald", Shoaling hypothesis, citing Hainault; to be checked against his own material while scripting.) The rival shoal theory, Six Fathom Shoal near Caribou Island, was backed by the Lake Carriers' Association. The evidence against both theories goes in the video too: divers found no sign of grounding on Six Fathom Shoal, and Shannon's 1994 photographs show no damage to the stern, propeller or rudder.
