# The script

About 1,900 words, which is about 13 minutes at the same pace as video 1. It is in 12 sections; record each as its own file, `01.mp3` to `12.mp3`.

"On screen" lines are for the edit. Do not read them aloud.

## 1. Midnight

*On screen: a lakefront city at night, rigging and flags straining in the wind. Water sliding along a dark street. Then the title.*

Buffalo, New York, is famous for snow. For blizzards that bury cars and shut the city down for days.

But the deadliest natural disaster in Buffalo's history did not come with snow. It came on an October night in 1844, and it came out of the lake.

Just before midnight on the eighteenth of October, the wind swung around and began to blow hard from the southwest, straight down the length of Lake Erie.

And the lake came with it.

In less than half an hour, the whole lower part of the city, everything south of the canal, was under water. Two feet deep in some streets. Eight feet in others. People woke up with the lake inside their homes.

By the time it was over, seventy-eight people were dead.

There was no hurricane. No dam broke. The lake had been pushed away to its far end, held there by the wind for days, and then let go.

And the same lake did the same thing again, last winter.

## 2. The bathtub

*On screen: map of Lake Erie, Toledo at the left end, Buffalo at the right, the long southwest-to-northeast line marked. A side view comparing the depths of the five lakes. Water in a bathtub tilting.*

To understand how a lake can do that, look at its shape.

Lake Erie is about two hundred and forty miles long, and it runs from southwest to northeast. Toledo, Ohio, sits at one end. Buffalo sits at the other.

It is also the shallowest of the five Great Lakes. On average it is only about sixty feet deep. Lake Superior, on average, is almost eight times deeper.

Shallow water is easy to push. And the strongest storm winds here often blow from the southwest, right along the lake, from one end to the other.

The National Weather Service describes Lake Erie as acting like "a giant bathtub." When a strong wind blows along it for hours, the water piles up at one end and drops at the other. In an extreme case, the weather service says, the water at Toledo and the water at Buffalo can differ by more than ten feet.

It works in both directions. When strong winds blow from the east instead, it is the western shore's turn, and the weather service watches the water at Toledo for flooding.

And when the wind stops, or turns, the water does not just settle back. It rocks.

That rocking has a name. A seiche. It is spelled S, E, I, C, H, E, and it is pronounced "saysh."

## 3. The city at the end of the canal

*On screen: 1840s Buffalo harbour crowded with canal boats and paddle steamers. A map of the Erie Canal from Albany to Buffalo. Small wooden houses close to the water.*

In 1844, Buffalo was one of the fastest-growing cities in America, and it owed almost everything to water.

The Erie Canal had opened in 1825, and Buffalo was where it ended. Grain and timber came across the lakes by steamboat. At Buffalo it was loaded onto canal boats and sent east, all the way to New York City.

People came the other way. Thousands of immigrants travelled west along the canal, and at Buffalo they waited on the waterfront for a steamboat to take them on across the lakes.

That waterfront was crowded, busy and low. Between the canal and the lake lay the lower part of the city: docks, warehouses, boarding houses, and small wooden homes where canal workers and their families lived.

Between that neighbourhood and Lake Erie stood a seawall. According to NOAA, it was fourteen feet high.

It was there to keep the lake out.

## 4. Two days of east wind

*On screen: the lake map with arrows pushing the water west, away from Buffalo. Low water at the Buffalo end, higher water at the Toledo end. Steamboats leaving the harbour at dusk.*

The trouble started with a wind that seemed harmless.

Before the storm, the wind had been blowing from the east. Writers at the time said it had "driven the water up the lake," away from Buffalo and toward the far, western end.

Think of the bathtub again. The lake was being tilted. Lower at Buffalo's end. Higher at Toledo's. And it was being held that way by the wind.

As long as the wind kept blowing from the east, nothing happened. The water stayed where it had been pushed.

Shortly before the storm, several steamboats left Buffalo, heading up the lake into that wind. Their names were the St. Louis, the Robert Fulton, the Indian Queen, and the Julia Palmer.

The Julia Palmer had about three hundred passengers on board.

## 5. The wind turns

*On screen: on the map, the wind arrows swing around to the southwest. A long surge moving east along the lake. The seawall at night, water rising against it, then over it.*

Then, at about midnight, the wind changed.

It swung around to the southwest, and it became a gale. Now it was blowing straight down the lake, from Toledo toward Buffalo.

All the water that had been held at the far end was suddenly released, and driven back the other way with the whole storm behind it.

It ran down two hundred and forty miles of lake, and it piled up at the one place it could go no further. The eastern end. Buffalo.

NOAA describes it as a twenty-two-foot seiche. It broke over the fourteen-foot seawall.

And in less than half an hour, a Buffalo newspaper reported, "the whole lower part of the city south of the canal" was under water, "from two to eight feet in depth."

## 6. The lower city

*On screen: dark flooded streets, wooden houses half under water, a lamp in an upstairs window, people on a roof, a house drifting.*

It was midnight. Most people in the lower city were asleep.

They woke to water coming in under the doors, then over the floors.

People climbed onto their roofs. One family held on to theirs while the flood carried them all the way to the Hydraulics, a district a mile and a half away.

The newspapers counted more than two hundred small buildings in the lower city destroyed outright.

The canal boats went too. The flood drove them ashore between Buffalo and Black Rock and left them stranded. Fifty of them, by one count. More than eighty, by another.

Fifty-three people died in Buffalo that night.

And the real number may be higher. Many of the people sleeping near the waterfront were immigrants passing through on their way west. Some of them may never have been counted.

## 7. Out on the lake

*On screen: a paddle steamer at night in huge waves, anchor chains taut, sparks from the funnel. A horse swimming toward a stormy shore.*

Out on the lake, it was worse.

The steamboats that had left Buffalo met the gale at its height. The Julia Palmer could not fight it. With her three hundred passengers, she was driven back down the lake, all the way to the bay off Buffalo, where her anchors finally caught and held.

Steamboats in 1844 burned wood, and the Julia Palmer had been fighting the storm for hours. Her wood began to run out.

A history of the Great Lakes, written decades later, says a horse swam ashore from her with a letter tied to its mane. The crew had burned all their wood, it said. They were "now burning the furniture."

A newspaper at the time told it a little differently. Two horses swam ashore from the Julia Palmer that morning, it said, "having washed or been thrown overboard."

Either way, Buffalo knew she was still out there. Still afloat. Still burning whatever would burn. She rode out the storm at anchor.

Others did not. Twenty-five more people died out on the lake that night.

## 8. Seventy-eight

*On screen: a grey morning over a wrecked waterfront, boats lying on dry land, broken houses. Then the number 78 on a dark card.*

In the morning, Buffalo saw what the lake had done.

Canal boats lay on dry land. Hundreds of small buildings were gone.

Fifty-three in the city. Twenty-five on the lake. Seventy-eight in all.

Writers in Buffalo called it the most disastrous flood "that has ever occurred since the city was founded." Some later accounts put the death toll far higher, as high as two hundred. Nobody can prove the true number now.

More than a hundred and eighty years later, New York Sea Grant still calls it the most destructive seiche ever documented in western New York.

Local historians call it the worst natural disaster in Buffalo's history.

And yet most people in Buffalo have never heard of it.

## 9. The real seiche

*On screen: a frame from the last video, the 1954 Chicago wave, with "seiche" crossed out and "meteotsunami" written in. Then an animation of Lake Erie rocking end to end, with a clock counting the hours.*

If you watched the last video on this channel, you have heard the word seiche before.

In 1954, a killer wave hit Chicago, and for decades it was called a seiche. It was not. It was a meteotsunami: one fast wave, over in minutes.

What hit Buffalo in 1844 was the real thing.

A seiche is not one wave. It is the whole lake tilting, and then rocking back and forth, end to end, the way water rocks in a bathtub after you climb out.

On Lake Erie, one full rock, from one end to the other and back again, takes around fourteen hours.

That is what makes a seiche so dangerous. It is slow. It can keep going long after the wind that started it. And the water that leaves one end of the lake has not gone anywhere. It is piled up at the other end, waiting to come back.

## 10. Why Buffalo forgot

*On screen: an early daguerreotype camera. Then Buffalo in deep snow. Then the breakwater from above, and the modern Canalside waterfront where the canal district stood.*

So why does almost nobody remember the worst night in Buffalo's history?

Part of the answer may be pictures. Photography had been invented only five years earlier. There are no known photographs of that night. No faces of the dead. Only newspaper columns.

Part of it is snow. Buffalo became the city of blizzards. Those are the storms people tell stories about.

And part of it is that Buffalo built its way out. In the 1860s, work began on a long breakwater out in the lake to shelter the harbour. The old canal district was rebuilt, and rebuilt again. Today the site of the old canal harbour is a waterfront district called Canalside.

The city changed. The lake did not.

## 11. It still happens

*On screen: a weather service chart from November 15, 2020: Buffalo's water level spiking up, Toledo's falling. Then December 2025: a drained harbour, bare lake bed, a car and a snowmobile sitting in the mud at Luna Pier, Michigan.*

On November 15, 2020, storm-force winds blew down Lake Erie from the southwest. For the first time since the remains of Hurricane Sandy in 2012, the weather service put the whole lake under a storm warning.

It measured a difference of fourteen feet between the water at Buffalo and the water at Toledo. Halfway along the lake, at Erie, Pennsylvania, the water climbed more than seven feet above its chart level and flooded the lakeshore, and some people in Millcreek Township had to be evacuated.

The bathtub had tilted again.

Then, on December 29, 2025, a winter storm hit the lakes with gusts of sixty to eighty miles an hour, and set off another seiche.

At the western end, the water drained away from the shore and left the lake bed bare. At Luna Pier, Michigan, the bare lake bed gave up things it had been hiding: a car, a snowmobile, a high school class ring.

At the other end, the water rose against Buffalo, and the weather service reported waves up to twenty feet high in the eastern part of the lake.

The same lake. The same wind. The same direction. A strong seiche like this comes every few years.

## 12. When the lake pulls back

*On screen: bare lake bed with water creeping back across it. Then the calm lake at Buffalo at dawn. End screen with the next video.*

There are two moments in a seiche that kill people.

The first is the one Buffalo met in 1844: the water arriving, in the dark, faster than anyone can get out of its way.

The second is quieter. It is the moment the lake pulls back. The water drains away, the lake bed is bare, and people are tempted to walk out and see what it has left behind.

But that water is not gone. It is piled up at the other end of the lake, and when the wind drops, it comes back.

So if you ever see Lake Erie, or any lake, pull far back from the shore after a storm, do not walk out after it. And if the weather service warns of lakeshore flooding where you live, believe it.

In 1844, the people of Buffalo went to sleep beside a lake that had been pushed away from them for days. Nothing told them it was only waiting.

Seventy-eight people died in Buffalo's worst natural disaster, and the city famous for snow almost forgot it ever happened. If you know someone who lives on Lake Erie, send them this.
