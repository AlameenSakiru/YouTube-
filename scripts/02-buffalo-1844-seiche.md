# The script

About 2,080 words, which is about 14 minutes at the same pace as video 1. It is in 12 sections; record each as its own file, `01.mp3` to `12.mp3`.

"On screen" lines are for the edit. Do not read them aloud.

## 1. Midnight

*On screen: a lakefront city at night, rigging and flags straining in the wind. Water sliding along a dark street. Then the title.*

Buffalo, New York, is famous for snow. For blizzards that bury cars and shut the city down for days.

But the deadliest natural disaster in Buffalo's history did not come with snow. It came on an October night in 1844, and it came out of the lake.

Just before midnight on the eighteenth of October, the wind swung around and began to blow hard from the southwest, straight down the length of Lake Erie.

And the lake came with it.

In less than half an hour, the whole lower part of the city, everything south of the canal, was under water. Two feet deep in some streets. Eight feet in others. People woke up with the lake inside their homes.

By the time it was over, seventy-eight people were dead.

There was no hurricane. No dam broke. The lake had been pushed away to its far end, held there by the wind for days, and then let go.

And the same lake did the same thing again, last winter.

## 2. The bathtub

*On screen: map of Lake Erie, Toledo at the left end, Buffalo at the right, the long southwest-to-northeast line marked. A side view comparing the depths of the five lakes. Water in a bathtub tilting.*

To understand how a lake can do that, look at its shape.

Lake Erie is about two hundred and forty miles long, and it runs from southwest to northeast. Toledo, Ohio, sits at one end. Buffalo sits at the other.

It is also the shallowest of the five Great Lakes. On average it is only about sixty feet deep. Lake Superior, on average, is almost eight times deeper.

Shallow water is easy to push. And Lake Erie's worst seiches come when strong winds blow from the southwest, right along the lake, from one end to the other.

The National Weather Service describes Lake Erie as acting like "a giant bathtub." When a strong wind blows along it for hours, the water piles up at one end and drops at the other. In an extreme case, the weather service says, the water at Toledo and the water at Buffalo can differ by more than ten feet.

It works in both directions. When strong winds blow from the east instead, it is the western shore's turn, and the weather service watches the water at Toledo for flooding.

And when the wind stops, or turns, the water does not just settle back. It rocks.

That rocking has a name. A seiche. It is spelled S, E, I, C, H, E, and it is pronounced "saysh."

## 3. The city at the end of the canal

*On screen: 1840s Buffalo harbour crowded with canal boats and paddle steamers. A map of the Erie Canal from Albany to Buffalo. Small wooden houses on low ground by the water.*

In 1844, Buffalo was one of the fastest-growing cities in America, and it owed almost everything to water.

The Erie Canal had opened in 1825, and Buffalo was where it ended. Grain and timber came across the lakes by steamboat. At Buffalo they were loaded onto canal boats and sent east, all the way to New York City. People came the other way, thousands of them, heading west across the lakes.

That waterfront was crowded, busy and low. South of the canal lay the lower part of the city: docks, warehouses, hotels, boarding houses, and small wooden homes where canal workers and their families lived. Across Buffalo Creek were more homes, on low, flat ground.

Out in the harbour stood a seawall and a stone pier, built to keep the lake in its place.

## 4. Days of east wind

*On screen: the lake map with arrows pushing the water west, away from Buffalo. Low water at the Buffalo end, higher water at the Toledo end. Four steamboats leaving the harbour at dusk.*

The trouble started with a wind that seemed harmless.

For several days before the storm, a stiff wind had been blowing from the east. A Buffalo newspaper explained afterwards what it had done. It "had driven the water up the lake," away from Buffalo and toward the far, western end.

Think of the bathtub again. The lake was being tilted. Lower at Buffalo's end. Higher at Toledo's. And the wind was holding it there.

On the evening of Friday, October 18, four steamboats left Buffalo, heading up the lake. The St. Louis. The Robert Fulton. The Indian Queen. And the Julia Palmer, with about four hundred passengers on board.

## 5. The wind turns

*On screen: on the map, the wind arrows swing around to the southwest. A long surge moving east along the lake. The seawall at night, waves breaking over it, huge stones shifting.*

Then, at about midnight, the wind changed.

It swung around to the southwest, and it blew, a later history says, "with a tremendous force never before or since known to the inhabitants of Buffalo."

Now it was blowing straight down the lake, from Toledo toward Buffalo. All the water that had been held at the far end was released, and driven back the other way. The newspaper put it simply. The water "came down before it as if a dam had broken away."

It ran down two hundred and forty miles of lake, and it piled up at the one place it could go no further. The eastern end. Buffalo.

It tore holes in the seawall and the stone pier, and carried stones weighing many tons ten and twenty feet. By the newspaper's measure, the lake rose twenty-two feet above where it had stood that evening.

And in less than half an hour, "the whole lower part of the city south of the canal" was under water, "from two to eight feet in depth."

## 6. The lower city

*On screen: dark flooded streets, wooden houses half under water, a lamp in an upstairs window, a house lifting off its foundation, two families on a split roof drifting in the dark.*

It was midnight. Almost everyone in the lower city was asleep. A later history called it "an avalanche of waters upon a sleeping community."

One man across the creek was woken by the noise of the wind. He guessed the water might rise, and ran to wake his family. Before he could get his trousers on, the water in his house was more than three feet deep.

On Main Street near the bridge, a witness saw it come "in one huge wave." At the corner of Main and Ohio Streets, the water stood six feet deep. At Huff's Hotel, at Main and Scott, six feet. In basements and on ground floors, there was almost no time to get out.

In one house, two families climbed to the second floor. The water followed them, and they climbed onto the roof. Then the flood lifted the whole house off its foundation and floated it away.

The roof split in two, with a family on each half. They drifted across the creek and over the flats, more than a mile in the dark. "The most fearful midnight voyaging," one account says, "that ever a man, wife and child underwent." Neighbours, risking their own lives, pulled them into the second storey of a house, just before the rafts were swept into Little Buffalo Creek.

Another family clung to their roof all the way to the Hydraulics, a mile and a half from where their house had stood.

## 7. Out on the lake

*On screen: a paddle steamer at night in huge waves, anchor chains taut, sparks from the funnel. A horse swimming toward a stormy shore with a paper tied in its mane.*

Out on the lake, the four steamboats met the gale at its height.

Off Dunkirk, the St. Louis broke her shaft, and four of her passengers were swept overboard and lost. The Robert Fulton lost two or three people overboard and was thrown up on the beach near Sturgeon Point, a total wreck.

The Julia Palmer could not fight it at all. She was driven helplessly back down the lake. At about six in the morning, opposite the foot of Main Street in Buffalo, her anchors finally caught and held.

She rolled and pitched there all that day. Steamboats in 1844 burned wood, and the Julia Palmer had been fighting the storm for hours.

Then a horse swam ashore from her, with a letter tied to its mane. They had burned all their wood, it said. They were "now burning the furniture."

On Sunday morning, when the water had gone down, the Julia Palmer came into the harbour. Everyone on board was safe.

## 8. Seventy-eight

*On screen: a grey morning over a wrecked waterfront, canal boats lying in the streets, a steamboat sitting in a field, broken houses. Then the number 78 on a dark card.*

In the morning, Buffalo saw what the lake had done.

Canal boats lay out in the streets, on Division, Eagle and Clinton. A steamboat, the Columbus, sat in a pasture two hundred feet from the creek. More than eighty canal boats had been driven ashore, and more than two hundred small buildings were gone. Across the creek, the newspaper said, there was "scarcely a house in its original position."

Most of the survivors had escaped in their nightclothes, with nothing else. The city opened the basement of the Mohawk Market as a shelter, and asked for clothing of any kind.

Then came the count. The newspapers said it was impossible to get "at any thing like accuracy." Their first list had thirty-nine dead, "besides several who are missing." Later records put the toll at fifty-three in Buffalo, and twenty-five on the lake.

Seventy-eight in all.

Today, New York Sea Grant still calls it "the most destructive documented seiche in Western New York." Local historians call it the worst natural disaster in Buffalo's history.

And yet most people in Buffalo have never heard of it.

## 9. The real seiche

*On screen: a frame from the last video, the 1954 Chicago wave, with "seiche" crossed out and "meteotsunami" written in. Then an animation of Lake Erie rocking end to end, with a clock counting the hours.*

If you watched the last video on this channel, you have heard the word seiche before.

In 1954, a killer wave hit Chicago, and for decades it was called a seiche. It was not. It was a meteotsunami: one fast wave, over in minutes.

What hit Buffalo in 1844 was the real thing.

A seiche is not one wave. It is the whole lake tilting, and then rocking back and forth, end to end, the way water rocks in a bathtub after you climb out.

On Lake Erie, one full rock, from one end to the other and back again, takes around fourteen hours.

That is what makes a seiche so dangerous. It is slow. It can keep going long after the wind that started it. And the water that leaves one end of the lake has not gone anywhere. It is piled up at the other end, waiting to come back.

## 10. Why Buffalo forgot

*On screen: an early daguerreotype camera. Then Buffalo in deep snow. Then the breakwater from above, and the modern Canalside waterfront where the canal district stood.*

So why does almost nobody remember the worst night in Buffalo's history?

Part of the answer may be pictures. Photography had been invented only five years earlier, and there are no known pictures of that night at all. Not a photograph. Not even a drawing. Only newspaper columns.

Part of it is snow. Buffalo became the city of blizzards. Those are the storms people tell stories about.

And part of it is that Buffalo built its way out. In the 1860s, work began on a long breakwater out in the lake to shelter the harbour. Buffalo still gets two or three seiches a year, but the breakwater takes most of their force. The old canal district was rebuilt, and rebuilt again. Today the site of the old canal harbour is a waterfront district called Canalside.

The city changed. The lake did not.

## 11. It still happens

*On screen: a weather service chart from November 15, 2020: Buffalo's water level spiking up, Toledo's falling. Then December 2025: a drained harbour and bare lake bed at the western end, waves breaking at Buffalo.*

On November 15, 2020, storm-force winds blew down Lake Erie from the southwest. For the first time since the remains of Hurricane Sandy in 2012, the weather service put the whole lake under a storm warning.

It measured a difference of fourteen feet between the water at Buffalo and the water at Toledo. Halfway along the lake, at Erie, Pennsylvania, the water climbed more than seven feet above its chart level and flooded the lakeshore, and several homes in Millcreek Township had to be evacuated.

The bathtub had tilted again.

Then, on December 29, 2025, a winter storm hit the lakes with gusts of sixty to eighty miles an hour, and set off another seiche.

At the western end, the water drained away from the shore and left the lake bed bare.

At the other end, the water rose against Buffalo.

The same lake. The same wind. The same direction. A strong seiche like this comes every few years.

## 12. When the lake pulls back

*On screen: bare lake bed with water creeping back across it. Then the calm lake at Buffalo at dawn. End screen with the next video.*

There are two dangerous moments in a seiche.

The first is the one Buffalo met in 1844: the water arriving, in the dark, faster than anyone can get out of its way.

The second is quieter. It is the moment the lake pulls back. The water drains away, the lake bed is bare, and people are tempted to walk out and see what it has left behind.

But that water is not gone. It is piled up at the other end of the lake, and when the wind drops, it comes back.

So if you ever see Lake Erie, or any lake, pull far back from the shore after a storm, do not walk out after it. And if the weather service warns of lakeshore flooding where you live, believe it.

In 1844, the people of Buffalo went to sleep beside a lake that had been pushed away from them for days. Nothing told them it was only waiting.

Seventy-eight people died in Buffalo's worst natural disaster, and the city famous for snow almost forgot it ever happened. If you know someone who lives on Lake Erie, send them this.
